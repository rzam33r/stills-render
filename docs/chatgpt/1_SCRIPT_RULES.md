# 1 — How the narration is written

The narration carries the film. The pictures only work because every
sentence gives them something concrete to show. These rules come from
studying narrated explainers that hold viewers for five minutes.

## Length and pace

- The voice speaks about 165 to 180 words a minute with almost no pauses.
- 5 to 6 minutes = 900 to 1,100 words. Never pad to reach a length.

## Structure

1. **Cold open (first 15 to 20 seconds).** Put the viewer in the scene:
   second person, present tense, something they can see, hear or touch,
   and a danger or stake they cannot see yet. No greeting, no channel name,
   no "in this video".
2. **The paradox and the question (by 30 seconds).** Say why the situation
   should have ended badly, then ask the film's question in one plain
   sentence. Promise that the answer is better than the viewer expects.
3. **Four to six chapters.** Each opens with a spoken title of 3 to 7
   words that sounds like a headline ("The water that looked safe").
   One idea per chapter, built from concrete moments, not summaries.
4. **A hand-off line at the end of each chapter**, short, that shows what
   the last idea could not solve and pulls into the next one. Two short
   sentences in contrast work well: "It got them started. It did not get
   them safe."
5. **A twist chapter** two-thirds of the way in: the thing most people get
   backwards. Announce it plainly ("Here is the twist most people miss.").
6. **The bigger truth.** A closing chapter that says what all the chapters
   add up to, in three or four strong lines.
7. **Back to the viewer.** End in the viewer's own life today, tied to the
   cold open, then one subscribe line that names what the channel uncovers.

## Sentences

- Short. Average 10 to 14 words. Mix in fragments for punch ("Simple.").
- Concrete nouns and actions every sentence: something a picture can show.
- Contrast pairs and lists of three: "Not one perfect solution, but layers
  of imperfect ones." "Through smell, taste, and the behaviour of animals."
- Repetition for emphasis at the end: two or three lines with the same
  opening ("They weren't protected by...").
- Ask a question when the viewer would be asking it.
- Talk to one viewer as "you".

## Truth

- Every fact must be one you would stand behind. Hedge what is not settled:
  "researchers estimate", "archaeologists believe", "the evidence suggests".
- Never invent a study, a statistic, a date, a place, a person or a quote.
- Prefer a vivid true detail to a dramatic vague one.

## Avoid

- "In this video", "let's dive in", "buckle up", "fun fact", "you won't believe".
- Long introductions, lists of dates, more than one number per sentence.
- Words a voice stumbles on: abbreviations, symbols, brackets, slashes.
